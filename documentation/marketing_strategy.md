## 1. The north-star metric: the demand test

The prototype's one job is to answer *"is the two-layer ship-design loop fun, and do people want
it?"* — see `prototype_scope.md`. Marketing-wise, **"do people want it?" = wishlists + demo
sentiment**, and the in-game **screenshot button + one clean 15-sec hook clip** (already in scope)
are the instruments that generate the shareable content that drives those wishlists. The
**share-the-result growth loop** (why presentation is a marketing engine, with the comparable-games
survey) lives in `designer_ux_research.md` — not repeated here.

## 2. Validated go-to-market funnel (evidence-backed)

The discovery path that demonstrably works for this **creative-builder niche** is:

**Public demo → short-form viral clips (TikTok/shorts) → Steam Next Fest → wishlists → launch.**

This is exactly the funnel our prototype is already built to feed (screenshot button + hook clip +
"present the build" camera). It is **not** trailer/storefront-copy led — the *toy* sells itself in
motion. Evidence: ShipShaper (below) started from **~5,000 wishlists off viral TikToks at
announcement**, then used Next Fest to convert buzz into a respectable wishlist base.

**Read:** a well-received small-scope creative/indie game in this space lands roughly in the
**low-thousands of sales + a few-hundred peak concurrent** band — viable, not a blockbuster. A
strong outcome for us looks like **clearing ~10k+ wishlists pre-launch** off the demo/clip funnel.

## 4. Wishlist yardsticks (industry rules of thumb)

- **~15,000 wishlists at launch** ≈ the **95th-percentile** "likely hit" threshold (How To Market A
  Game). Below that is still commercially fine for a small-scope solo game; above it signals breakout
  potential.
- Typical conversion: **~10–20% of wishlists** convert to sales in the first weeks. So ~10k wishlists
  ≈ ~1–2k first-week sales, building over time.

## 5. Honest caveats (don't over-read the evidence)

- **ShipShaper's traction is partly the man, not the concept.** Tomas Sala had pre-existing
  credibility + a fanbase (*The Falconeer* / *Bulwark*), refined demo-making skill, a publisher, and
  lucky viral momentum. How To Market A Game explicitly calls his result **exceptional, not
  replicable**. As a less-established dev, **assume the concept must carry more of the weight** — our
  job-sim wrapper + functional interior is the depth ShipShaper deliberately lacks, and is our wedge.
- **Search is US/English-biased** and won't surface an unannounced or non-English project. "No twin
  found" = no *visible, indexed* twin (see the competitive scan that produced these benchmarks).
- **Numbers are estimates / snapshots** and move; re-check before any decision that leans on them.

## 6. Case study: Tiny Glade's marketing playbook (what to copy)

Tiny Glade is our **feel/quality north-star** (see `ships_and_stations_design.md`); its **marketing**
is just as instructive. Two-person studio **Pounce Light**, **zero paid marketing**, launched with
**~1,375,441 wishlists → ~616k copies in under a month** (a freak **~45%** wishlist conversion vs the
~10–20% norm), **$15 held firm**, **~96–97% Overwhelmingly Positive**, ~10k concurrent on launch day.
The playbook that got there:

- **Engineer virality FIRST, build the Steam page SECOND.** They posted short WIP clips from **Oct
  2021** — months before a Steam page, sometimes without even naming the game — and had **~800k
  wishlists *before* the Next Fest demo**. Reverse of the usual order. → *For us: the screenshot
  button + 15-sec hook clip (`prototype_scope.md`) aren't launch-time polish; start posting clips the
  moment the hull-shaping verb looks good.*
- **The clip is the product pitch.** Every video "screams *this'll be fun to build in, play me*";
  first viral TikTok hit **9.7M views**. The author's blunt rule: **only beautiful/cute games get
  traction on TikTok/Twitter.** → *Our virality bet rides entirely on the hull-shaping + "present the
  build" lighting reading as instantly satisfying in ~3 seconds.*
- **The product reason it converted: "a lot from little effort."** Each action feels rewarding /
  hand-crafted despite procedural gen — this is the **same pillar we already adopted** in the quality
  north-star. The marketing only works because the *toy* delivers. ("90% game / 10% marketing.")
- **Email newsletter to hold fans before there's a wishlist button** — **28% CTR vs ~1% on social.**
  Cheapest audience-retention tool; start one at first viral clip.
- **Coordinated launch beat** — sync a **themed festival + new trailer + Steam page publish + name
  reveal** into one spike → **10k wishlists in 24h** and Steam's algorithm rewards the burst.
- **Festival fit + Next Fest demo with daily build challenges** — themed prompts ("House on a Hill,"
  "Tiny Wizard Tower") **every day** drove UGC and repeat engagement; top-10 demo, ~20k/day wishlist
  spike. → *Our contracts map perfectly onto a daily-themed-build challenge during our Next Fest.*
- **Streamer outreach as a cadence** — a real email pitch, contacting people **weekly**; hit their
  30k-wishlist target two months early.

**What does NOT transfer cleanly (be honest):**
- **Their numbers are a top-0.1% outlier** — do not set 1M-wishlist / 45%-conversion targets. The
  realistic yardstick stays §3–§4 (clearing ~10k+ wishlists is a strong outcome for us).
- **Their virality edge was near-zero UI friction** — pure cozy visual candy. **Our game is more
  UI-complex** (the exact concern flagged on the ShipShaper note in `ships_and_stations_design.md`).
  That is a **virality headwind**: clips must hide the complexity and lead with the satisfying *shape*
  act, or they won't read in 3 seconds.
- **Positioning question (open):** Tiny Glade is unambiguously *cozy*; we straddle **cozy creative
  builder ↔ job/management sim.** Which festivals/audiences we court (Wholesome vs sim/management)
  is an open marketing decision — parked in `open_questions.md` territory, flagged here.
